# Maximum minimum modulus of a distinct covering system

## Description of constant

A **distinct covering system** is a finite collection of residue classes

$$
\mathcal C=\{a_i+n_i\mathbb Z:1\le i\le k\},
\qquad a_i\in\mathbb Z,\quad 1<n_1<\cdots<n_k,
\qquad \bigcup_{i=1}^{k}(a_i+n_i\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z.
$$

The classes may overlap; distinctness refers to their moduli. Define

$$
C_{90}:=\max_{\mathcal C}\min_{1\le i\le k}n_i
       =\max_{\mathcal C}n_1.
$$

The family is nonempty: for example, the classes $0\pmod 2$, $0\pmod 3$, $1\pmod 4$, $1\pmod 6$, and $11\pmod {12}$ cover all integers. Hough's theorem [H2015] bounds the minimum modulus uniformly, so the attainable minimum moduli form a nonempty bounded set of positive integers. Consequently the maximum exists and is attained.

As of October 2026, the strongest known bounds are

$$
44\le C_{90}<616000,
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
44\le C_{90}\le615999.
$$

The lower bound 44 is an unrefereed, machine-checked construction [M2026], marked with an asterisk below; the previous record is 42, from Owens's MS thesis [O2014], which later literature cites as the best known construction [B2024, §2]. The exact value remains unknown. Erdős's original minimum modulus problem asked whether the minimum could be arbitrarily large [E1950]. Hough settled that question negatively; determining the optimal finite bound remains open.

## Additional comments and links

- **Analytic precursor.** Filaseta, Ford, Konyagin, Pomerance, and Yu [FFKPY2007] established restrictions involving the reciprocal sum of the moduli and uncovered density. These are important precursors, but they do not supply an additional unconditional numerical upper bound in the table.
- **Restricted variants.** Cummings, Filaseta, and Trifonov [CFT2025, Theorem 1.1] prove a minimum modulus at most 118 when every modulus is squarefree. The extra hypothesis is essential to that result; 118 is not an upper bound for the constant defined here. Hough and Nielsen [HN2019] prove that some modulus is divisible by 2 or 3; this does not say that a modulus is equal to 2 or 3.
- **Further reading.** Balister's survey [B2024], §2, gives recent context and agrees with the bounds above.
